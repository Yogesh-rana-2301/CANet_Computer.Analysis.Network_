# Transmission Media and Wireless Transmission

Transmission media is the physical path that carries signals between network devices.

It is broadly classified into:

- Guided media (wired): signals travel through a solid medium.
- Unguided media (wireless): signals travel through air or space.

## 1) Guided Transmission Media (Wired)

### Twisted Pair Cable

Two insulated copper wires twisted together to reduce electromagnetic interference.

Types:

- UTP (Unshielded Twisted Pair)
- STP (Shielded Twisted Pair)

Common categories:

- Cat5e: up to 1 Gbps (typical)
- Cat6: up to 1 Gbps, 10 Gbps for shorter distances
- Cat6a: up to 10 Gbps (longer distances)

Pros:

- low cost
- easy installation
- widely available

Cons:

- limited distance (about 100 m for Ethernet segment)
- more susceptible to noise than fiber

Typical use:

- LAN cabling in homes, offices, campuses

### Coaxial Cable

Copper core with insulation, shielding, and jacket.

Pros:

- better shielding than UTP
- supports longer distances than basic twisted pair in some use cases

Cons:

- bulkier and less flexible
- less common in modern Ethernet LANs

Typical use:

- cable TV networks
- broadband internet access (DOCSIS)

### Fiber Optic Cable

Uses light pulses through glass/plastic fiber.

Types:

- Single-mode fiber (SMF): long distance, high capacity
- Multi-mode fiber (MMF): shorter distance, cost-effective for local high speed

Pros:

- very high bandwidth
- very low attenuation
- immune to electromagnetic interference
- better security against electromagnetic tapping

Cons:

- higher installation and transceiver cost
- more delicate splicing/termination requirements

Typical use:

- backbone networks
- data centers
- metro and long-haul links

## 2) Wireless (Unguided) Transmission

Wireless transmission uses electromagnetic waves through free space.

### Radio Waves

- Can travel long distances.
- Often omnidirectional.
- Can penetrate walls (frequency dependent).

Use cases:

- broadcast radio
- low-frequency communication systems

### Microwaves

- Higher frequency than basic radio.
- Often directional and line-of-sight.
- Can carry high data rates.

Use cases:

- point-to-point backhaul links
- cellular infrastructure
- satellite communication

### Infrared (IR)

- Short-range communication.
- Does not pass through walls.
- Can be line-of-sight or diffuse.

Use cases:

- remote controls
- short-range device links

### Satellite Transmission

Signals are uplinked to a satellite and downlinked to another location.

Pros:

- very large geographic coverage
- useful where wired infrastructure is unavailable

Cons:

- higher latency (especially geostationary satellites)
- weather attenuation at some frequencies
- higher equipment/service costs

Use cases:

- remote connectivity
- maritime and aviation internet
- broadcast distribution

## Common Wireless Technologies

- Wi-Fi (IEEE 802.11): local area wireless networking.
- Bluetooth: short-range personal area networking.
- Cellular (4G/5G): wide-area mobile communication.
- NFC: very short-range contactless communication.
- Zigbee/Thread: low-power IoT communication.

## Propagation and Channel Effects

Wireless signals are affected by environment.

### Reflection

Signal bounces off surfaces (walls, buildings, ground).

### Refraction

Signal bends when moving through media of different density.

### Diffraction

Signal bends around obstacles.

### Scattering

Signal disperses due to rough surfaces or particles.

These effects can cause multipath propagation, where multiple copies of a signal arrive at different times.

## Wireless Impairments

- Path loss: signal weakens with distance.
- Interference: overlapping signals from other devices.
- Fading: signal strength fluctuates over time.
- Noise: random unwanted signals.
- Shadowing: obstacles reduce signal level.

Mitigation methods:

- adaptive modulation and coding
- channel selection and planning
- MIMO and beamforming
- forward error correction
- proper antenna placement

## Comparing Wired and Wireless

| Factor                    | Wired                        | Wireless                               |
| ------------------------- | ---------------------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| Mobility                  | Low                          | High                                   |
| Reliability               | High                         | Medium (environment dependent)         |
| Installation              | Physical cabling required    | Faster deployment in many cases        |
| Security (physical)       | Easier to control physically | More exposed over air                  |
| Speed consistency         | More stable                  | Can fluctuate with interference        |
| Distance (single segment) | Depends on cable type        | Depends on frequency, power, obstacles |

## Media Selection Guidelines

Choose medium based on:

- required data rate
- distance and coverage area
- budget (equipment + installation + maintenance)
- environment (EMI, weather, physical hazards)
- mobility needs
- security and compliance requirements
- scalability for future growth

Practical rule of thumb:

- fiber for high-speed backbone and long distance
- twisted pair for cost-effective LAN edge
- Wi-Fi/cellular when mobility and rapid deployment are priorities

## Security Considerations

### Wired

- protect patch panels and switch rooms
- use physical access control
- disable unused ports and apply NAC where possible

### Wireless

- use strong encryption (WPA2/WPA3)
- strong authentication and segmented SSIDs
- rotate credentials/keys and monitor rogue APs
- perform regular site surveys and spectrum checks

## Quick Summary

Transmission media determines how signals travel, what speeds are practical, and how reliable communication will be. Wired and wireless each have strengths, and real network design often combines both to balance performance, cost, mobility, and resilience.
